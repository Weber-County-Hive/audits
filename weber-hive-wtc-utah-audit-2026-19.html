The Work Got Done. The Paperwork Didn't Keep Up.

A legislative audit of World Trade Center Utah found the state-funded nonprofit met its contracts, but its main success number is self-reported and its spending reports fell short.

Issued by: Office of the Legislative Auditor General Audited: World Trade Center Utah and GOED Chief officers: Jonathan Freedman (WTC Utah) · Commissioner Jefferson Moss (GOED)

Posted Oct. 17, 2026 · Updated Oct. 17, 2026

New to this? Read it in plain words About 500 words, with two pictures.

The short version

  • Legislative auditors reviewed World Trade Center Utah, a private nonprofit that helps Utah companies sell overseas and draws foreign investment. The state paid it about $2.2 million in fiscal 2025 and nearly $7.2 million from 2021 through 2025.
  • The audit found WTC Utah appears to have done the work its two state contracts asked for, and that its trade mission fees for government officials were in line with its contract.
  • Its main result number, "new trade opportunities," counts prospective sales along with real ones, is reported by the businesses themselves, and is not consistently checked. The audit says it "may overstate actual economic impacts."
  • WTC Utah fell short on three reports, including its yearly filing with the State Auditor. The audit says it had not filed one since 2024. It says it fixed that in July 2026.
  • The GOED contract never required WTC Utah to report what it spent. That contract ends Dec. 31, 2026, and a new one is being negotiated.

HOW THIS PIECE GRADES THE STATE'S OVERSIGHT OF THIS MONEY

The contracted work appears to have been delivered and no misuse was found. But for years the state paid without asking how the money was spent, and the one result number rests on what businesses chose to report.

What the audit found

World Trade Center Utah (WTC Utah) is a nonprofit, founded in 2006, that helps Utah businesses sell into other countries, brings in foreign investment, and hosts foreign delegations. It works closely with the Governor's Office of Economic Development (GOED), which oversees both of its state contracts.

Two legislative leaders' questions drove the audit: Are the state funds given to WTC Utah being spent efficiently and effectively? And are the fees WTC Utah charges to go on trade missions appropriate?

The auditors looked mainly at fiscal 2025, the latest complete year. Their answer to the first question was mixed. WTC Utah "appears to have carried out the activities required under these contracts and achieved the intended contract deliverables." But the auditors found four areas to improve, and wrote: "WTC Utah did not have processes in place that ensured reporting compliance, and limited oversight by prior GOED leadership allowed deficiencies to continue."

On the second question, the auditors "did not find that WTC Utah is charging fees in violation of its current contract."

Where the state money goes

The state pays WTC Utah through two separate deals. The audit notes that state funding grew in 2023, when the GOED contract added work, and again in 2024, to open and run a trade office in South Korea.

The "new trade opportunities" number

Most of what WTC Utah reports to the state are counts of activity, such as meetings held or grants given. The audit calls these outputs. It reports only one outcome, a number meant to show results: "new trade opportunities," or NTO.

NTO adds together three things: actual sales, prospective sales and actual investment, as reported by businesses in impact statements and surveys after they work with WTC Utah. The audit found three problems with that:

  • It includes deals that have not happened yet and may never happen.
  • It relies on what businesses say, and "strong supporting documentation is not consistently obtained."
  • As a result, "NTO figures may overstate actual economic impacts."

The audit recommends WTC Utah follow up with businesses for proof of completed deals, and that GOED review whether its contract measures show whether the program is working.

What WTC Utah says about NTO

In its response, WTC Utah says reporting is voluntary in nearly all cases, that companies often can't share confidential financial records, and that pushing for them could discourage the reporting the number depends on. It also says NTO is "a success of a company, not an economic development organization," and that it tracks the number as a sign of program performance, not as its own standalone success.

It agreed to report NTO in three tiers: prospective, actual and publicly announced (backed by a press release, public filing or other public record). It also agreed to check in every year with every company that reported NTO. Its timetable: tier definitions by Oct. 31, 2026; the first full follow-up and 2026 numbers by tier by Dec. 31, 2026. The plan notes it is "subject to further discussion with GOED."

Three reports that fell short

The audit found three places where WTC Utah's reporting did not meet the requirements:

PASS-THROUGH REPORT

The contract requires WTC Utah to say "how Grantee used funds or will use funds." The 2025 annual report it gave GOED did not include those details.

GRANT REPORTING

The GOED contract asks for grant details. The quarterly reports listed the number of grant recipients, but not the grant money available, the number of companies awarded, or the NTO from the grants. WTC Utah says it reported some of this and folds grant results into its overall NTO on purpose, so the same deal isn't counted twice.

STATE AUDITOR FILING

Utah law requires nonprofits that get $25,000 or more in public money to file an accounting report with the State Auditor each year. The audit says WTC Utah met that threshold every year but had not filed since 2024. WTC Utah says it uploaded its past financial audits on July 28, 2026, and started a compliance calendar on July 30, 2026.

The audit names two causes: WTC Utah had no process to make sure the reports were done, and "prior GOED leadership provided limited oversight of pass-through funds and approved final payments to WTC Utah despite incomplete reporting."

WTC Utah says the gap happened during a change-over in its finance department, "did not involve the misuse of public funds," and that an independent accounting firm audits its books every year. GOED holds a seat on its board.

Trade mission fees

Some people the auditors spoke with questioned why WTC Utah charges people to join trade missions when the state already pays it to run them. One asked whether that meant WTC Utah was paid twice.

The GOED contract, as amended in 2023, says WTC Utah books and pays flights, hotels and fees for the Governor and up to two members of the Governor's team. Everyone else on the delegation, including state employees and elected officials, pays their own way, and WTC Utah "may also charge trade mission delegation members a participation fee." WTC Utah sets aside $200,000 of its $525,000 GOED payment for two trade missions a year.

The audit found no fee charged against the contract. But it says the new contract should make clear that state money and fees don't pay for the same thing twice. It also notes that in the 2026 General Session, the Legislature gave GOED $250,000 in one-time money for travel and lodging of executive and legislative officials on trade missions.

WTC Utah's response: "no activity, service, or cost is funded twice." It says its two state deals pay for different work.

The audit says the fee question is discussed further in the GOED audit, Report 2026-18. Read our case file on that audit.

The five recommendations

1.1 · GOEDReview the contract's performance measures for WTC Utah and use them to guide future funding.
1.2 · WTC UtahFollow up with businesses for documents confirming completed sales, investments and other results.
1.3 · WTC UtahStrengthen its process for meeting contract and State Auditor reporting rules, with regular management review.
1.4 · GOEDSpell out in the new contract how the money is to be spent.
1.5 · GOEDRequire regular expense reports tied to each key contract deliverable.

All five were accepted.

What WTC Utah and GOED said

WTC Utah (letter from President and CEO Jonathan Freedman, Aug. 14, 2026)
Accepted both recommendations aimed at it and said it plans to finish them by Dec. 31, 2026, "pending further conversations with GOED." It highlighted its public-private model and its weekly tariff briefings: 57 online sessions through June 30, 2026, with 440 different people attending. It cautioned that "highly prescriptive line-item budgeting and reporting" could slow its ability to shift resources when business needs change. Named contacts: Ben Kolendar, chief of staff (recommendation 1.2), and Heather Garcia, chief financial officer (1.3).
GOED (letter from Commissioner Jeff Moss, Aug. 21, 2026)
Accepted all three recommendations aimed at it. Deputy Commissioner Tara Rosander is named on each. GOED says it has already reviewed the measures and written new ones, that the proposed new contract says how money is to be spent (Attachment D), and that it requires quarterly reports on key deliverables (Attachment B). On all three, it says a draft contract has been provided and GOED is "attempting to execute a new contract."

Common questions

Did the audit find money was misused?
No. It found the contracted work appears to have been done and the fees were within the contract. The problems were measurement and reporting.
Is this the same as the One World Trade Center in New York?
No. WTC Utah is a Utah nonprofit and part of a worldwide network of trade centers. It is not the New York building.
Why audit a nonprofit?
Because it receives public money. The audit was requested to check whether state funds were spent well and whether its trade mission fees were appropriate.
Does the state still fund WTC Utah?
Yes. The audit says the Legislature has typically given the pass-through money every year. GOED's contract runs through Dec. 31, 2026, and a new one was in draft when the audit came out.
Is this connected to the GOED audit?
Yes. Both reports came out Sept. 15, 2026, and both involve GOED's oversight of money it passes on to outside groups. This report points readers to the GOED audit for more on trade mission fees.
