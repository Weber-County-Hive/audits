STATE AUDITOR REPORT USU25SP · JULY 29, 2025

Up to $2.8 Million, and Nobody Checked

A State Auditor review found a state-funded manufacturing nonprofit's president used program money for a for-profit company he controlled, big bonuses and vacations, while two state agencies each assumed the other was watching.

Issued by: Office of the Utah State Auditor Reviewed: iMpact Utah; GOEO; Utah State University; University of Utah State Auditor: Tina M. Cannon

Posted Oct. 17, 2026 · Updated Oct. 17, 2026

New to this? Read it in plain words About 500 words, with two pictures.

The short version

  • The State Auditor found that iMpact Utah, a nonprofit paid to help Utah manufacturers, used an estimated $1.8 million to $2.8 million of public program money for things that did not serve the program.
  • The biggest piece: up to $2.1 million went to or for a for-profit company in which iMpact Utah's president was the majority shareholder. He also received bonuses of $525,000 over three years, trips charged to the company card and $9,958 in political donations made by the nonprofit.
  • The state office that passed the money along (GOEO, now GOED) and Utah State University did not check how it was spent, and iMpact Utah had no working board.
  • iMpact Utah never filed the financial reports required of nonprofits getting public money, and nobody told the State Auditor it should have.
  • All the agencies accepted the recommendations. iMpact Utah was set to close July 31, 2025. GOEO said it was working with the Attorney General's office in an "ongoing, active investigation." We found no public report of charges or money recovered as of Oct. 17, 2026.

HOW THIS PIECE GRADES THE OVERSIGHT OF THIS MONEY

Public money moved through three public bodies to a nonprofit with no working board, no required financial filings and no one checking the spending, for years, until a hotline tip.

What the review found

The Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP) is a federal program, started in the late 1980s, that helps U.S. manufacturers become more competitive. In Utah, the University of Utah receives the federal grant. Because the federal money must be matched, the Legislature also puts in state money, which goes through the Governor's Office of Economic Opportunity (GOEO) to Utah State University (USU).

The universities passed most of the money to iMpact Utah (legally the Business Training & Resource Center), a nonprofit that trained and advised manufacturers for a reduced fee. The State Auditor's office opened this review after a complaint to its hotline alleging misuse of public funds.

This was a limited review, not a full audit. It looked at a sample of iMpact Utah's transfers, credit card charges and other records for 2023 and 2024, interviewed current and former employees, and looked at what the state agencies did. The report warns that "had we extended the period of our review... other matters may have come to our attention."

Why "GOEO" here and "GOED" on other pages?

The office was called the Governor's Office of Economic Opportunity (GOEO) when this report came out in July 2025. H.B. 475 (2026) renamed it the Governor's Office of Economic Development (GOED). It is the same office, and Jefferson Moss led it in both years.

The report treats all of it, federal money, state match and fees, as public program money, because federal grant rules cover all three. iMpact Utah earned little else, so the auditors counted any improper spending as misuse of public funds.

Finding 1: Nobody checked

The auditors found GOEO and USU "did not exercise adequate oversight," which "allowed subrecipient iMpact Utah (IU) to misuse Project funds and related Program Income without detection for several years."

  • GOEO was named in the budget bills as the agency in charge of the money. Apart from asking for a yearly status report, it "did not perform any substantive oversight activities." It read the budget language as an order to move the money, not to oversee it.
  • USU's agreements with GOEO told it to "ascertain that expenditures made are for the purposes appropriate," but USU staff did not read that as requiring them to oversee iMpact Utah. USU's own agreements with iMpact Utah said only that funds "will be spent by the parties in support of the program."
  • The report sums it up: "it appears that neither entity assumed responsibility for overseeing how the funds were used."
  • Until July 1, 2024, no specific state law governed these direct-award grants. A new law (Utah Code 63G-6b) took effect for only the last six months of the review.

Proper oversight, the report says, would have included reviewing spending, reading audited financial statements, checking that iMpact Utah had a working board, and making sure it filed its State Auditor reports. The misuse "may expose the state to federal scrutiny, including potential cost disallowances and reimbursement requirements."

Finding 2: How the money was used

iMpact Utah "did not have a functioning board of directors." The report says that with no outside oversight, its president, who is not named in the report, used the nonprofit's money "for personal benefit."

With bonuses, his pay and benefits averaged $518,823 a year from 2022 to 2024, "more than double" what leaders of similar-sized nonprofits received. The auditors call about $750,000 of it excessive, and say it likely went beyond what the IRS considers "reasonable compensation."

Trips, personal costs and political gifts

  • At least $35,715 in vacations with his wife in 2023 and 2024, charged to the company card: Hawaii, Las Vegas, Florida and more, including a helicopter tour, a snorkel cruise, luaus and shows. The auditors "could see no legitimate business purpose."
  • About $2,563 in other personal costs: a massage, subscriptions, Amazon purchases, a haircut and exercise equipment.
  • $9,958 in donations from iMpact Utah to three state campaigns and one federal campaign in 2023 and 2024. The report notes the IRS bars 501(c)(3) nonprofits from campaign activity. The report does not name the campaigns.

Adding it up, the auditors estimate $1.8 million to $2.8 million "was used inappropriately."

Finding 3: Reports never filed

Utah law (Utah Code 51-2a-201.5) says a state agency that gives a nonprofit public money must require it to say when it has received $25,000 or more, and must then tell the State Auditor. The nonprofit must file financial reports based on how much it got.

Neither USU nor the University of Utah put that rule in their agreements with iMpact Utah, told the State Auditor about the payments, or made sure iMpact Utah filed. Since iMpact Utah got more than $1 million a year, it should have had an independent CPA audit and filed it with the State Auditor "for the past several years." The report says iMpact Utah appeared unaware of the rule, and that the misuse "may have been prevented or detected sooner" if the reports had been filed.

The same rule shows up in the World Trade Center Utah audit, where a nonprofit also fell behind on this filing, and in the GOED audit, which counted 14 nonprofit grantees not current with the State Auditor. GOED audit case file · World Trade Center Utah case file

The recommendations

GOEO (now GOED)Set up real oversight under Utah Code 63G-6b; require recipients of MEP and other pass-through money to monitor the groups they pay; make sure the fiscal 2026 money does not go to a group that may owe money back or no longer exists.
USUBuild stronger grant procedures and check on the groups it pays; work with the University of Utah so monitoring meets federal rules.
GOEO and USUDecide whether trying to recover the misused money is appropriate or feasible, and consider referring the matter to law enforcement.
University of UtahWork with GOEO and USU on the federal impact and with federal MEP officials on a resolution.
USU and University of UtahFollow the $25,000 reporting law: tell nonprofits about their State Auditor filings, watch that they file, and tell the State Auditor who they pay.

What the agencies said

GOEO (Executive Director Jefferson Moss, July 22, 2025)
Accepted the recommendations. Said its ability to set monitoring rules was limited because it "historically had limited legal authority" over pass-through money and contracts only with its recipients, not their subrecipients. Said it "has not, and will not, distribute funds to an entity after an audit shows grant funds were inappropriately used," and will step up oversight. Noted that whistleblower tips are the most common way this kind of misconduct is found, and that GOEO got the same tip as the auditor.
Utah State University (July 16, 2025)
Said nothing in the state MEP contract told it the money was tied to federal funds or required monitoring. Will work with state agencies so awards say who must be monitored, and will review its procedures. Will work with GOEO and the Utah Attorney General's office on recovery and with GOEO on law enforcement referrals. (Its letter says "unused" MEP funding where the recommendation says "misused.")
University of Utah (July 22, 2025)
Said its MEP center had been in constant contact with the federal MEP program, issued iMpact Utah a stop-work order on March 26, 2025, and got full marks in its May 2025 federal annual review, including financial records. It acknowledged the State Auditor's position on the reporting law, will build ways to comply, and wants to talk with the State Auditor and legislators about the law's intent.

Common questions

Was anyone charged with a crime?
The report does not say anyone was charged. It recommends considering a law enforcement referral. GOEO told the Deseret News it was working with the Attorney General's office in an "ongoing, active investigation." We found no public report of charges as of Oct. 17, 2026. An audit finding is not a court finding.
Why isn't the president named here?
The State Auditor's report does not name him, and we follow the report.
Was the money paid back?
The report asked GOEO and USU to decide whether recovery is "appropriate or feasible." We found no public report of money recovered as of Oct. 17, 2026.
Does Utah still fund the MEP program?
Yes. H.B. 4 (2025) set $2.8 million for the alliance in fiscal 2026, and the 2026 budget summary lists $1.5 million ongoing for it. The federal grant goes to the University of Utah.
How does this connect to the GOED audit?
The 2026 GOED audit points to this case as an example of what can happen when pass-through money isn't monitored. Both involve the same state office.
